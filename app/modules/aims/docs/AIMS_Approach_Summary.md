# The AIMS approach: regulating receptivity in patient-provider vaccine conversations

**Authors:** John Parrish-Sprowl, Angus Thomson, Rodger D. Johnson, Susan Parrish-Sprowl  
**Journal:** Frontiers in Public Health (2023)  
**DOI:** 10.3389/fpubh.2023.1120326

---

## Abstract

The World Health Organization named vaccine hesitancy a leading global health threat of modern time. Addressing this public health issue requires a multi-front strategy, one such strategic effort is training health care professionals to respond to reluctant patients/caregivers or those who refuse vaccines.

AIMS (Announce, Inquire, Mirror, and Secure) is designed to help health care professionals engage in more productive conversations with patients/caregivers to secure trust, a key behavior leading to higher vaccination rates.

---

# 1. Introduction

The World Health Organization named vaccine hesitancy one of the top 10 threats to global health in 2019. The definition has evolved from:

> “a delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccination despite availability of vaccination services”

to:

> “a motivational state of being conflicted about, or opposed to, getting vaccinated.”

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) are often the most trusted source of vaccine information, but modern social media ecosystems have increased uncertainty and misinformation.

AIMS (Announce, Inquire, Mirror, Secure) was developed to help HCPs facilitate more productive vaccine conversations in difficult healthcare environments.

Feedback from trainings involving HCPs from more than 20 countries suggested:
- 93% felt empowered to proactively discuss vaccination
- 80% believed the skills would help their daily practice

# 2. Background

Traditional “tell and sell” communication approaches often fail.

Research increasingly suggests:
- Data dumping can backfire
- Respectful dialogue works better
- Motivational interviewing techniques improve engagement
- Empathy improves receptivity

Key themes from the literature include:
- reflective listening
- relational trust
- caregiver autonomy
- empathy
- conversational process over raw information transfer

The paper argues communication is a **bioactive process**:
- social interaction affects biology
- stress states affect receptivity
- empathic communication improves health outcomes

Examples cited:
- reduced anxiety
- improved wound healing
- better adherence to treatment
- improved vaccine effectiveness associated with positive mood

The paper heavily references:
- Polyvagal Theory
- Interpersonal Neurobiology
- neuroplasticity
- mirror neurons
- epigenetics

# 3. The AIMS Approach

AIMS is designed to increase receptivity by intentionally creating a calmer, more trusting conversational environment.

The acronym stands for:

1. Announce
2. Inquire
3. Mirror
4. Secure

---

## Announce

The clinician:
- calmly and confidently announces it is time for vaccination
- uses a friendly, non-paternalistic tone
- avoids overexplaining initially

Example concept:
> “It’s time for Nathaniel’s vaccines today.”

Research suggests many caregivers accept vaccination after a simple presumptive recommendation.

If hesitation appears, move immediately to Inquire.

---

## Inquire

The clinician:
- asks open-ended questions
- avoids argumentative framing
- talks *with* the caregiver rather than *at* them

Preferred question styles:
- “What concerns do you have?”
- “How are you feeling about the vaccines?”

Avoid:
- accusatory “why” questions

Inquiry serves several functions:
- demonstrates interest
- gathers tailored information
- avoids introducing unnecessary fears
- empowers the caregiver

---

## Mirror

The clinician reflects the caregiver’s concerns back to them.

Goal:
- ensure the caregiver feels understood
- build trust
- reduce defensiveness

The paper emphasizes:
> The person needs to “feel felt.”

Mirroring may repeat several times before moving on.

Example structure:
> “It sounds like you’re worried about side effects and want to make the safest decision possible. Did I get that right?”

---

## Secure

Secure is about strengthening the relationship, not forcing compliance.

At this point:
- tailored information can be offered
- the caregiver is more receptive
- trust is reinforced

Key principles:
- respect autonomy
- maintain relationship
- preserve future dialogue

Example concepts:
- “This is your decision.”
- “We both want what’s safest for your child.”
- “I’m here to support you.”

The paper explicitly states:
> Secure is about the relationship, not about persuading the person regarding vaccination.

---

# 4. Research Findings

The paper cites studies suggesting:
- AIMS behaviors can be reliably identified
- healthcare workers trained in AIMS report:
  - increased empathy
  - increased counseling confidence
  - increased vaccine acceptance conversations

A later study involving 1,200 participants from more than 100 countries found:
- 61% reported increased empathy/confidence/vaccine acceptance
- 90% reported changing how they approach vaccine conversations

---

# 5. Key Conceptual Themes

The paper repeatedly emphasizes:

## Trust over persuasion
The relationship is central.

## Receptivity over argument
The goal is to reduce reactance and defensiveness.

## Process over content
How something is said matters as much as what is said.

## Nervous system regulation
The clinician’s calmness influences the caregiver’s state.

## Autonomy support
People resist coercion but respond better to respectful collaboration.

# Critiques / Observations

The paper:
- strongly leans on Polyvagal Theory and interpersonal neurobiology
- sometimes extrapolates beyond strong empirical evidence
- blends established communication science with more speculative neuroscience framing

However, the practical communication guidance itself is:
- coherent
- clinically usable
- strongly aligned with motivational interviewing principles

The most operationally useful aspects are:
- reflective listening
- autonomy support
- avoiding premature fact-dumping
- structured conversational pacing
