Technical Reflections on the Design and Development of AIMSBot

My role in the AIMSBot project focused on translating a communication framework into a software system that could support realistic practice, meaningful feedback, and consistent assessment. While the underlying AIMS framework is grounded in health communication theory, implementing it in software required a series of design decisions that exposed important questions about how communication training can be operationalized in an AI-supported environment.

One of the primary challenges was converting a qualitative framework into a system capable of producing reliable and repeatable behavior. Concepts such as Announce, Inquire, Mirror, and Secure are intuitive to experienced communicators, but software requires explicit definitions, rules, and decision boundaries. As development progressed, many implicit assumptions within the framework had to be made explicit. Questions such as what constitutes successful mirroring, how different communication behaviors should be scored, and when a learner should advance from one stage of the conversation to the next became central design concerns.

A second challenge involved balancing conversational flexibility with pedagogical consistency. Large language models are highly effective at producing natural and nuanced dialogue, making them well suited for simulating vaccine-hesitant patients. However, they are less reliable as a sole mechanism for assessment and instruction. For this reason, AIMSBot adopted a hybrid architecture in which generative AI was responsible for conversational realism while deterministic software components managed scoring, state transitions, coaching logic, and system guardrails. This division allowed the system to remain conversational while providing learners with consistent feedback.

The project also highlighted the importance of state management in educational conversational systems. Effective coaching depends not only on what a learner says in a given turn, but also on what has occurred throughout the conversation. The system therefore needed to track which concerns had been raised, which had been explored, which had been mirrored, and what opportunities remained for further communication. Maintaining this continuity became a foundational requirement for meaningful coaching.

Perhaps the most interesting insight from the project was that AIMSBot is fundamentally a teaching system rather than a chatbot. While much public discussion of conversational AI focuses on generating realistic dialogue, the educational value of AIMSBot depends on its ability to guide learning. This shifted the design focus away from simply producing believable responses and toward questions of feedback, assessment, progression, and learner experience. In practice, the coaching system often proved more challenging to design than the simulated patient itself.

The development process also revealed a persistent tension between realism and structure. A realistic patient may change topics, express multiple concerns simultaneously, or respond unpredictably. An educational system, by contrast, requires opportunities for reflection, assessment, and measurable progress. Much of the design effort involved balancing these competing objectives so that conversations remained authentic enough for practice while still supporting the learning goals of the AIMS framework.

From a broader perspective, the project demonstrates the value of combining communication theory, educational design, and artificial intelligence within a single system. The most significant contribution of the technical work was not simply the application of generative AI, but the development of mechanisms that translate a relational communication framework into a structured learning experience. In doing so, AIMSBot provides a practical example of how AI can be used to support communication training while maintaining pedagogical consistency, reliability, and learner-centered design.
